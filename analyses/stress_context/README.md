# Stress timing and tic expression evidence reconciliation

Project NeuroFreedom | Bounded research update | 9 October 2026

## Decision

**Do not use a universal immediate stress-to-more-tics model. Keep delayed stress prediction and acute context-dependent expression as separate hypotheses.** The available studies do not establish that a single biological process causes both patterns, explains multiweek waves, or produces remission. No effect estimates were pooled.

A reporting audit also found an unresolved inconsistency in the principal acute experiment. It should qualify subsequent use of that study, without discarding its observations.

This is public-science research, not a clinical protocol. No participant information was collected, no treatment or home stress experiment is proposed, and no repository was changed by this artifact's author.

## Question and method

**Hypothesis tested against published evidence:** higher stress consistently increases contemporaneous visible tic frequency, so prospective and laboratory studies estimate the same relationship.

**Prediction:** positive associations should recur across immediate observations and delayed severity ratings after aligning exposure, outcome and timing. Strong context-specific null or inverse results would weaken this universal model.

**Executed method:** read the project protocol, current status, cycle brief, evidence ledger and foundations; retrieve primary studies; compare population, exposure, cadence, outcome and statistical target; actively inspect discordant studies; audit a printed test statistic. This was a targeted reconciliation, not a systematic review or new analysis of participant data. The completed synthetic periodicity benchmark was not rerun.

## Structured primary study extraction

### Lin and colleagues 2007

[Primary author manuscript](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3073143/) | DOI 10.1111/j.1469-7610.2006.01687.x | PMID 17300554 | February 2007 issue

- **Design and sample:** two-year naturalistic specialty-clinic cohort; 45 cases aged 7–17, including 37 with TS/chronic tics and eight OCD-only cases; 41 controls. Tic-model analytic N was not separately verified.
- **Cadence:** clinician YGTSS tic scores and parent PSS-P monthly; youth DLSS and clinician contextual-threat YCGSI four-monthly. Visits varied: 4–26, mean 21. The text inconsistently lists depression ratings in both schedules.
- **Estimand:** mixed-effects structural-equation association of the nearest recorded preceding stress measure/latent construct with subsequent severity; not a fixed experimentally assigned stress dose.
- **Result:** modest positive stress-to-future-tic association after age/depression adjustment; prior-symptom adjustment reduced effects but retained significance, with adjusted estimates not shown. Medication-change sensitivity analysis reportedly preserved directions. No verified tic-path coefficient or confidence interval is extracted here.
- **Limits:** shared reporting, heterogeneous lags, treatment selection, small referred cohort and incomplete follow-up; random intercepts do not by themselves establish a purely within-person causal effect. Temporal order does not eliminate unmeasured confounding. No held-out forecast validation was reported.

### Buse and colleagues 2016

[Publisher full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2016.00084/full) | DOI 10.3389/fpsyt.2016.00084 | PMID 27242554 | 17 May 2016

- **Design:** 31 youths aged 7–17 (21 TS, 10 chronic tic disorder); 28 valid tic records. Randomized-order within-person six-condition experiment: three situations crossed with suppression instruction; five-minute blocks. Speech-based social stress, concentration and relaxation differ beyond stress. Video/audio raters were hypothesis-blind, not demonstrated condition-blind.
- **Estimand:** immediate task/instruction differences in observed counts. Free-tic means (SD) per five minutes, stress/concentration/relaxation: 8.29 (16.40)/13.43 (14.84)/17.11 (18.28).
- **Result:** situation F(2,54)=3.1, p=.053; post-hoc stress–relaxation p=.017; stress–concentration nonsignificant. Published interaction F(2,54)=3.1, p<.044 is inconsistent (audit below).
- **Physiology:** heart rate and skin conductance rose; cortisol's nonparametric omnibus p=.099, stress–relaxation p=.117. Valid N differed: cortisol 20, heart rate 26, conductance 25. Urge improvement was not demonstrated.
- **Limits:** unmeasured suppression effort, attention, social evaluation, missing data, carryover and multiple comparisons. The experiment does not isolate stress physiology or measure delayed exacerbations. Low counts under both instructions cannot distinguish impaired suppression from a floor/occlusion effect.

### Hoekstra and colleagues 2004

[Publisher abstract](https://www.psychiatrist.com/jcp/association-small-life-events-self-reports-tic-severity/) and [publisher PDF](https://www.psychiatrist.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/11020_association-small-life-events-self-reports-tic-severity.pdf) | DOI 10.4088/JCP.v65n0320 | PMID 15096084 | March 2004

- **Design/cadence:** 12-week prospective weekly questionnaires; 25 pediatric and 32 adult participants; qualifying analyzed groups 24 and 28.
- **Estimand:** contemporaneous and one-week-lagged associations between event frequency and subjective tic severity. Event counts are not equivalent to perceived stress.
- **Result:** adult same-week r=.268, p<.001; no overall adult one-week-lag association. Only 21% of pediatric and 18% of adult participants had significant individual relationships; signs varied.
- **Limits:** short individual series, subjective outcomes, multiple individual tests and different pediatric/adult event instruments. Nonsignificance is not equivalence. Nevertheless, this prospective counterevidence blocks generalizing one positive cohort result to a universal weekly trigger.

### Conelea and colleagues 2011

[PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21658680/) and [publisher preview](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0005796711001070) | DOI 10.1016/j.brat.2011.05.006 | PMID 21658680 | online 25 May 2011; August issue

- **Design:** ten youths aged 9–17, four randomly sequenced conditions: free tics, reinforced suppression, stress, stress plus suppression.
- **Estimand/result:** immediate observed frequency did not differ between stress alone and baseline; stress plus suppression yielded more tics than suppression alone.
- **Limits:** tiny selected sample; full block durations, covariance and numeric contrasts not verified from the accessible abstract/preview. This supports an interaction candidate, rather than a general instantaneous increase. A task-related competing demand could mimic a stress-specific suppression effect.

### Conelea and colleagues 2014

[Primary manuscript](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4175308/) | DOI 10.1177/0145445514528239 | PMID 24662238 | March 2014

- **Design:** eight children with tic and anxiety disorders; synchronized video and heart rate during two laboratory tasks. High/low heart-rate intervals were defined within the recordings, rather than assigned biological exposure.
- **Estimand/result:** tics/minute during high versus low heart rate: 7.01 versus 6.72 across the experiment, p=.58; during speech, frequency was lower in high-heart-rate intervals.
- **Limits:** selected anxiety sample, eight participants, physiological association rather than randomized autonomic manipulation. Heart rate is not a complete stress measure. Together with the other laboratory studies, this weakens a simple immediate arousal-to-tic-count mapping; it cannot exclude slower effects.

### Related cohort safeguard

[Lin and colleagues 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2843763/), PMID 19833320, describes the same-sized Yale 45-case/41-control two-year cohort and cites the earlier work. It adds infection modeling and reports altered stress coefficients. Treat it as an overlapping-cohort extension, not independent replication. Its monthly telephone/four-monthly visit description also warns against interpreting every monthly rating as an in-person observation.

## Reconciliation

The universal hypothesis is not supported by this comparison. However, the apparently opposite signs are **not an identified biological sign reversal over time**: the studies changed exposure, measurement, setting and participant selection together with timescale. There is no bridge dataset here measuring the same independently assessed exposure and multiple outcomes over both horizons.

Three nonexclusive explanations remain:

1. **Delayed susceptibility plus immediate expression control.** Stress-related processes could alter later propensity while attention, task engagement or suppression temporarily changes visible output. This is a coherent model, not a demonstrated mechanism.
2. **Contextual measurement effects.** An observed count combines propensity, expression opportunity and detection. A clinical severity rating also incorporates dimensions beyond frequency. Converting both to a standardized effect size would not create a shared causal target.
3. **Confounding and heterogeneity.** Sleep, mood, treatment and baseline symptom persistence could influence exposure and outcome; different people or stressors could have different effects. A pooled positive association can coexist with weak within-person prediction.

**What remains unsupported:** stress reduction necessarily changes tic biology; fewer visible tics means lower distress; a later return to baseline proves rebound; a quiet interval identifies remission; or any of these results establishes a weeks-long oscillator.

## Executed numerical reporting audit

The [publisher PDF, page 4](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2016.00084/pdf) and HTML agree on the printed interaction values. Under the ordinary F distribution, F=3.1 with 2 and 54 degrees of freedom gives **p=.0531528127**, not p<.044. Even F values rounding to 3.1 at the nearest tenth imply p approximately .05082–.05559.

SciPy and the independent closed-form survival probability for numerator df=2 agreed. Inputs are the potentially rounded printed statistic and degrees of freedom, not underlying data. This identifies a reporting inconsistency; it does not determine which printed value is wrong or reproduce the participant-level ANOVA. Retain the pattern descriptively and mark the interaction's significance unresolved pending original statistical output or corrected reporting.

## Discriminating predictions and next decision

These are proposed tests for governed existing-data research; none has been executed here.

| Candidate | Discriminating prediction | Finding that weakens it |
|---|---|---|
| Delayed stress contribution | Prespecified earlier stress improves later independently rated burden prediction beyond recent burden, age, time and treatment; survives within-person centering and forward validation | No incremental held-out improvement, or signal limited to between-person differences |
| Immediate context/attention effect | A context term improves momentary count prediction; its effect differs from delayed burden and varies with task engagement | Comparable observation windows show no context dependence |
| Shared-rater bias | Associations are stronger when exposure and outcome share an informant than when outcome is independently rated | Similar temporal association under independent ascertainment |
| Reverse/common-cause explanation | Future-stress negative controls, alternative lags, prior tics and measured common causes explain the association | Prespecified antecedent effect survives these checks and replicates |

**Methods requirement:** estimate momentary counts and later severity separately; record actual timestamps and lookback windows; separate between-person from within-person components; prespecify lag windows rather than select the largest association; model recent severity/autocorrelation; examine missingness and symptom-driven treatment changes; avoid using future observations to label a real-time transition. Simply adjusting for a post-exposure variable may block mediation or induce bias, so covariates require an explicit temporal causal diagram.

**Next bounded decision:** first audit publicly documented cohort instruments and available timing metadata for a feasible, independent-rater, within-person lag analysis. The minimum prerequisite is an actual access route and documented sampling sufficient for the chosen lag. If only monthly symptom ratings and sparse contextual ratings exist, restrict the claim to that resolution; do not fit daily transitions or infer multiweek periodicity. No investigator contact or controlled-data application has occurred.

## Quiet periods and remission

For this project, a quiet period is an observation over a stated setting and interval. Remission requires an explicit threshold, duration, ascertainment and treatment-status definition. Neither acute stress experiment establishes remission or recovery biology.

[Pappert and colleagues 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14557563/) provides an ascertainment warning: 31 of 56 childhood-video patients returned as adults for standardized five-minute recordings; 90% showed tics, including half of those reporting themselves tic-free. The selected cohort and attrition prevent interpreting 90% as a universal persistence estimate. The useful lesson is that self-report and brief observation can disagree; neither a single quiet recording nor absence by recollection proves sustained absence. The planned remission-definition harmonization remains a separate necessary workstream.
