# Exercise and tic expression in young people

Evidence checked on 8 October 2026. This note contains only published aggregate research, with no patient-specific data or family context. It is a scientific evidence summary, not a treatment recommendation or activity prescription.

## Main interpretation

Small acute studies report reduced observed tic frequency during exercise and in short post-exercise observations. A larger observational study found a modest association between greater activity and lower tic severity. These findings neither establish a universal benefit nor exclude individual worsening. Temporal sequence alone does not establish causation.

The measurement window matters: tics observed during movement, tics observed shortly afterward, week-level severity, and symptoms hours later or the next day are different outcomes. The studies below do not establish whether a particular activity causes delayed exacerbations. Observed tic counts also do not fully measure discomfort, effort, impairment, or well-being.

## Four core primary studies

### Nixon and colleagues 2014

[Reduced Tic Symptomatology in Tourette Syndrome After an Acute Bout of Exercise](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24778432/) · DOI 10.1177/0145445514532127

- **Sample and design:** 18 young people with Tourette syndrome; acute within-participant observational comparison.
- **Window:** Video-based tic rates during exercise versus pre-exercise and post-exercise interviews. The authors' 2020 paper describes the earlier reduction as sustained below baseline for up to 30 minutes.
- **Finding:** Tic rates were lower during exercise and remained lower afterward; self-reported mood and anxiety also improved.
- **Limit:** Small sample and different observation contexts. Short-term reduction does not establish lasting benefit or address later-day or next-day changes.

### Jackson and colleagues 2020

[Tic frequency and behavioural measures of cognitive control are improved in individuals with Tourette syndrome by aerobic exercise training](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32492517/) · DOI 10.1016/j.cortex.2020.01.029

- **Sample and design:** 18 enrolled, 17 analyzed; approximately ages 10–20. Within-participant kickboxing versus Tai Chi comparison; major psychiatric comorbidities were excluded.
- **Window:** Approximately 10-minute exercise sessions. Tic counts used 5-minute video samples from pre-exercise interviews, exercise, and post-exercise interviews; cognitive testing occurred immediately before and after exercise. The verified methods excerpts do not specify an exact elapsed time to the post-exercise tic sample.
- **Finding:** Both activities reduced tic frequency during exercise; reduction was greater and persisted longer after kickboxing.
- **Limit:** Small, selected sample and short laboratory observations. Findings do not isolate every contribution of movement, attention, expectations, or context.

### Pringsheim and colleagues 2020 online and 2021 print

[Physical activity, sleep and neuropsychiatric symptom severity in children with tourette syndrome](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32372272/) · DOI 10.1007/s00787-020-01552-1

- **Sample and design:** 110 children with tic disorders; observational study.
- **Window:** Wrist accelerometry measured activity and sleep continuously for seven days, alongside symptom questionnaires. This was not a time-aligned test of tics before and after individual exercise bouts.
- **Finding:** Greater activity had a small inverse association with tic severity, approximately r = −0.22. Objective sleep duration and efficiency did not correlate with symptom severity; subjective sleep quality did.
- **Limit:** Association cannot establish direction. Tic severity, comorbidity, or other factors could influence activity levels.

### Conelea and colleagues 2014

[Is There a Relationship Between Tic Frequency and Physiological Arousal](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24662238/) · DOI 10.1177/0145445514528239

- **Sample and design:** Eight children with co-occurring tic and anxiety disorders; laboratory stress tasks, not an exercise trial.
- **Window:** Synchronized heart rate and tic observations during discussion and public-speaking tasks, comparing higher- and lower-heart-rate intervals.
- **Finding:** Tics did not generally increase during higher-heart-rate periods; during the speaking task, they were less frequent at higher heart rates.
- **Limit:** Very small sample. Heart rate, perceived stress, performance pressure, and tic expression cannot be treated as interchangeable measures.

## Newer combined intervention evidence

A [2025 randomized trial of Mindful Energy Balance Exercise](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12550066/) enrolled 135 children aged 4–12; 121 completed and entered the reported completer-restricted primary analysis. Assessments occurred at baseline and weeks 6, 12, and 24. Adding a 12-week program to standard care improved tic outcomes relative to standard care alone. The program combined mindful movement, breathing, body awareness, and attention practices. It does not isolate ordinary physical exercise or determine acute or delayed responses to strenuous activity. Lack of an active comparison program and exclusion of non-completers limit interpretation. It would therefore be inaccurate to state categorically that no randomized movement-related studies exist.

## Boundaries on causal interpretation

Exercise intensity, fatigue, sleep, attention, excitement, and social or performance demands are distinct variables. They may coincide with an activity, but these studies do not establish which causes a given change in tics. They do not demonstrate that elevated arousal invariably worsens tics, that short-term tic reduction implies reduced burden, or that a change after activity represents lasting neurological harm. Mechanistic explanations should remain hypotheses unless directly tested.
