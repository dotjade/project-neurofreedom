# Gaming, screen use, and tic expression

Public-evidence note. Checked 8 October 2026. This is a research summary, not an individual assessment, diagnosis, or intervention protocol.

## What the evidence supports

Some media experiences can change tic expression in the moment. This does not establish that gaming causes Tourette syndrome, permanently worsens it, or that removing a particular game for several weeks treats the underlying condition. Individual responses and content differ. A repeated everyday association can be worth discussing with a clinician without establishing its mechanism.

## Direct studies and limits

1. **Primary experimental research:** [Raz et al., European Journal of Neurology, 2024; first published November 2023](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/ene.16120). Twenty children aged 7–16 were analyzed in movie/custom-game experiments; a game finding was replicated in 36 children aged 7–15. Tic frequency rose during reward anticipation. These were short-term measurements, not a Minecraft study or a weeks-long withdrawal/reintroduction trial. Dopamine was a proposed explanation, not directly measured. The authors explicitly request long-term research.

2. **Primary observational research:** [Mohamed et al., Italian Journal of Pediatrics, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11770938/). A retrospective case-control study included 342 children with tic disorders and 270 controls. More reported daily screen time was associated with higher tic-severity scores. Retrospective design and caregiver-reported exposure limit causal interpretation. Children with ADHD or autism were excluded, limiting generalizability. The association cannot determine whether screens preceded worsening, whether symptoms encouraged screen use, or whether another factor affected both. Mechanistic speculation in the discussion should not be treated as a demonstrated finding.

## Interpreting changes across weeks

Short laboratory effects cannot explain a multiweek trajectory by themselves. Natural fluctuation, school stress, holidays, illness, treatment changes, and changing routines can coincide with screen changes. Reduced screen use may also change bedtime, physical activity, family interaction, or frustration. These are alternative or mediating explanations, not findings established for a particular person. Greater immersion during difficult periods is another possible direction of influence. Attention and engagement can change tic visibility without demonstrating a change in the underlying disorder.

3. **Authoritative clinical guidance:** [NHS: Tourette syndrome](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/tourette-syndrome/). Stress, excitement, and tiredness can trigger tics. Guidance emphasizes consistent routines, physical activity, and a calm bedtime without electronic devices beforehand. This supports checking sleep and arousal when interpreting screen associations; it does not supply a validated abstinence duration or universal screen-time threshold for tics.

## Interest, control, and support

4. **Authoritative diagnostic framework:** [WHO: Gaming disorder](https://www.who.int/standards/classifications/frequently-asked-questions/gaming-disorder). Strong enthusiasm or frequent conversation about games is insufficient. Relevant concerns are impaired control, gaming displacing important activities, persistence despite harm, and significant functional impairment, usually evident for at least twelve months. Assessment should consider sleep, school, relationships, flexibility, and the person's own distress without assigning a label from limited observations.

Supportive discussion and predictable boundaries should avoid blame or punishment for involuntary tics. Deliberately provoking symptoms to prove an association is unnecessary. Research on social-media-associated functional tic-like presentations should not be substituted for evidence about established tic disorders.

## Access and scope

Public full-text study pages and official guidance were accessible for verification; an initial PMC retrieval was gated before an alternate publisher route succeeded. No participant-level data or supplementary reanalysis was examined. This focused search is not a systematic review.
