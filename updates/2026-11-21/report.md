## Question, prediction and comparison

The [November 16 audit](../2026-11-16/report.md) kept participation separate from tic severity and found no basis in the three sources examined for pooling across age and severity. Does the new tic-adapted Child Occupational Self Assessment (COSA-t) study supply a change endpoint, or primarily professional evidence that its items are relevant for pediatric assessment? Prediction: clinician relevance and use are supported; a repeatable change score or a bridge to adults is not. Classify evidence by who reported what and when, whether the same children were assessed repeatedly, and whether any age/severity linking was tested. Use the unadapted scale to check what may and may not transfer.

| Original source | Evidence supplied | What it does not establish for COSA-t |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Schug et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/43061991/) | 19 CBIT-trained occupational therapists rated the importance of items on a 0–10 scale; reported item ratings span 5.95–8.95. Eighteen took part in five focus groups. Clinicians described how occupation-based assessments could surface everyday impacts and inform planning. These are clinician judgments and reports, not children’s performance or change scores. | Child-reported relevance/comprehension, score stability, responsiveness, clinically meaningful change, efficacy, or equivalence across ages or severity are not established by the abstract's reported design. The 5.95–8.95 range is not a patient score, confidence interval, or treatment effect. |
| [Kramer et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.5014/ajot.2010.08142), original COSA | 502 children with disabilities, ages 6–17, completed self-ratings of competence and value; item and person fit and dimensionality analyses provided evidence about score structure, with mixed external validity. | Indirect evidence for the adapted instrument only. Different item content and a different target population prevent assuming the same score structure or performance in tic disorders, let alone persistent severe adult symptoms. |

A [University of Florida poster](https://ufdcimages.uflib.ufl.edu/AA/00/09/86/72/00001/Schug_Thaila.pdf) by overlapping authors describes 19 therapists, similar eligibility, and focus groups awaiting analysis. Treat it as probably overlapping preliminary material, not independent replication; do not combine samples. The journal abstract reports both clinician survey and focus groups in one study.

**Result and decision:** Retain the new report as pediatric clinician-informed item relevance, not evidence of patient change or treatment effect. Keep the original scale's psychometric evidence marked indirect. The next evidence gate is an instrument-specific study asking children whether items reflect their concerns, then reporting measurement error and whether repeated COSA-t scores detect meaningful changes in participation. Keep adult functioning separate and look for direct evidence in adults across severity, including persistent severe tics, rather than translating child scores. No new data collection, treatment recommendations or individual inference.
